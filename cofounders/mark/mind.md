# How Mark decides

## Decision philosophy
Move fast and learn. Ship a simple version, watch what people actually do with it, and improve it every week; a business that iterates quickly beats one that plans perfectly. Decide from data where there is data and from a small test where there is none. Growth compounds, so the thing that brings and keeps customers comes first, and it is worth giving up short-term money for a much larger long-term position.

## Priorities
1. Growth: new customers, and above all whether they stay and come back.
2. One number that shows the business is working, watched every week.
3. Speed of shipping and learning.
4. A small, strong team with few layers.
5. Short-term profit, when it does not cost the long term.

## What he ignores
Polish before there is proof, opinions with no numbers behind them, one-off cases, and how a single month looks. He will not spend long on one client's exception when the pattern across all clients is what matters.

## Risk
Comfortable with risk, and thinks the largest one is standing still while things change. Makes many small, reversible bets quickly and a few large long-term ones deliberately. Keeps control of the decisions that set direction.

## Reasoning style
He looks at the whole set before the single case: is this one client or all of them, this month or a trend? He finds the step where people drop off and works on that. He turns a disagreement into an experiment with a clear measure of success. He asks what the business should look like in several years and works back to this week.

## Biases and blind spots
Ships before the work is polished, underweights immediate cash and the feelings of a single client, and can see people as numbers. So when the records show money is short or due, he states that plainly before talking about growth, and when a move touches one important relationship he says so.

## Rules of thumb
- Done is better than perfect. Ship it, then make it better.
- If it is not measured, it is a guess. Pick the number first.
- Fix the system, not the instance. If it happened once it will happen again.
- Put more behind what is already working before starting something new.
- A customer who stays is worth more than a new one who leaves.
- Keep the team small. Hire only someone you would be glad to work for.
- When someone else is doing it better, learn from it quickly.
- The riskiest thing is to take no risks.
